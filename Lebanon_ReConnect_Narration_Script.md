## 1. Title

Hi, I'm Maher Bekdash, and this is my LebNet Tech Fellows final project: Beirut ReRoute, which grew into Lebanon ReConnect. In one sentence, this project designs, and proves in simulation, an AI-driven path from Beirut's fragmented public transport to a regionally-reconnected Lebanon. Over the next few minutes I'll walk through why this problem matters, how my thinking on it evolved three separate times, exactly how the AI methodology works, what the real results actually are, and what I learned building it.

## 2. Agenda

Here's the shape of the talk. First, the problem — why Lebanon's transport system needed this project. Second, and I want to spend real time here, the evolution of thought: this project did not arrive at its final form on day one, and I think the path it took is as instructive as the destination. Third, the methodology, split into two layers. Fourth, the real results, all pulled from actual code runs, not estimates. Fifth, what's genuinely novel here and, just as important, what I'm not claiming. And finally, the action plan and my honest reflection on the process.

## 3. The Problem

Let's start with why this matters. The World Bank estimates 650,000 vehicles enter the Greater Beirut Area every single day, and congestion costs Lebanon roughly 5 percent of its GDP. Lebanon has the highest transport-related CO2 emissions per capita in the Levant. And even though walk-accessibility to some kind of stop today measures at 92.2 percent, that remaining 7.8 percent is around 235,000 real people with no practical way to reach public transport. The deeper issue is that Beirut's transport is a patchwork: informal vans fill gaps organically, and the new 2024 OCFTC formal bus network — eleven lines — runs in the exact same mixed traffic as every private car, with no dedicated lanes, no signal priority, and critically, no feeder network connecting underserved neighborhoods to it. So the real question this project asks is: what would it actually take to fix that, concretely and provably, and how far could that ambition scale — from one neighborhood, all the way up to the whole region?

## 4. Aim

So here's the aim, stated as precisely as I can state it. Design, and prove in simulation, an AI-driven path from Beirut's fragmented transit to a regionally-reconnected Lebanon. I want to be specific about what that means in practice. This is not a diagnosis — I'm not just producing another report that says "Beirut has a transit problem." It's not a wish list either. It's an AI method that actually designs a route network using real optimization, a simulation that actually tests that network against the status quo using real road data, and real, sourced regional data quantifying what reconnecting Lebanon regionally could concretely mean for Beirut's role in the wider region.

## 5. Evolution of Thought — Part 1

Now, the evolution of thought — part one. This started as simply wanting to fix Beirut's buses: I cared specifically about routes, congestion hotspots, coverage gaps, and modern technology like IEEE 802.11p, or V2X, on Beiruti roads. Before committing to a direction, I generated four scoped candidate ideas: a transit-desert detector, a congestion-plus-V2X corridor simulation, an AI trip planner for the informal bus network, and a mobility-equity dashboard. I chose the congestion-plus-V2X direction: predict congestion hotspots with machine learning, then simulate a V2X-style adaptive traffic signal controller on one corridor. But before writing a line of code, I did a scope reality check — reinforcement-learning-based signal control, layered on top of a full separate ML prediction pipeline, is genuinely ambitious for solo work in three weeks. So I descoped deliberately: one corridor instead of the whole network, an existing reinforcement-learning library instead of a custom controller I'd have to build and debug from scratch, and one prediction model instead of two. The idea stayed the same; the risk went down.

## 6. Evolution of Thought — Part 2

Evolution of thought, part two. At this point I got direct pushback on the framing, and it was the right pushback: "I don't just want to predict congestion — I want to propose a system that solves the current shortcomings. Congestion prediction is a subtopic, used as a black box." That single sentence restructured the entire project. The AI headline stopped being congestion prediction and became network design itself: a coverage-optimization algorithm — specifically, a Maximal Covering Location Problem, or MCLP — that actually proposes a feeder route network, rather than just diagnosing where the problems are. The travel-time and congestion model got demoted to a supporting utility that feeds realistic estimates into the design and simulation steps — never the star of the show. And a simulation got added: a rule-based transit-signal-priority simulation, representing what real V2X and 802.11p communication would enable, that validates the proposed network against today's system. This is where Layer B was born — what I now call Beirut ReRoute.

## 7. Evolution of Thought — Part 3

Evolution of thought, part three, and this is the big one. The ask expanded again: "I want to envision all of Lebanon, not just Beirut" — ports, rail, trams, metro, international lines, Beirut as a regional hub connecting to Damascus, Amman, Cyprus, and Turkey, regardless of budget. My honest reality check: physically simulating ports, ships, and a national rail network is not buildable solo in a matter of days. But the ambition itself was completely legitimate, and when I actually researched it, it turned out to be extremely well-timed rather than speculative. There is a real, dated Jounieh-to-Cyprus ferry launching in 2026. There is a real Turkey-Syria-Jordan-Saudi Arabia agreement reviving the historic Hejaz Railway, signed across 2025 and 2026. And Lebanon is already a signatory to the Arab Mashreq International Railway agreement. So instead of building one project that tried to do everything and risked finishing none of it, I reconciled the ambition into two honestly-scoped layers — which I'll show you next. And around this same point, I discovered that Layer B, the Beirut pilot, was already substantially implemented in real, tested code — so the work pivoted directly into extending that codebase for the new national layer.

## 8. System Architecture — Two Layers

Here's how the two layers fit together. Layer A, Lebanon ReConnect, is the national and regional vision: a lightweight network graph of Lebanon's ports, its historic and currently-being-revived rail lines, and the 2026 regional ferry, evaluated using graph centrality analysis rather than physical simulation. It answers one specific question: how much more central could Beirut become, regionally, if these dormant and planned links were restored? This layer is scoped to days, not weeks, and it's explicitly topological rather than engineering-grade. Layer B, Beirut ReRoute, is the AI-designed, simulated pilot: the coverage-optimization algorithm designs a real feeder network for underserved Beirut neighborhoods, connecting them to the 2024 OCFTC trunk lines, and a signal-priority simulation tests that proposed system against today's, on real OSM roads and real geocoded stops. This layer is scoped to one region, but it's fully executable and fully tested. And critically, the two are connected by a phased action plan — Layer B is explicitly Phase 1 of Layer A's larger vision, not a separate, disconnected project.

## 9. Methodology — Layer B

Let's get into how Layer B actually works, technically, in four stages. Stage one is data acquisition: real OpenStreetMap road and walk networks, WorldPop population density data, the crowdsourced Lebanese Bus Routes dataset for informal vans, and OCFTC's nine formal lines, which I hand-transcribed from transitapp-dot-com and geocoded through OpenStreetMap's Nominatim service — 191 stops total, 157 successfully resolved. Stage two is accessibility scoring: an H3 hexagonal grid scores every cell's walk-time to its nearest stop today, which gives the status-quo baseline of 92.2 percent population-weighted coverage. Stage three is the actual AI network design: a Maximal Covering Location Problem, solved with PuLP and OR-Tools, chooses feeder stops that maximize newly-covered underserved population for each trunk line — this is the real "AI designs a network" step, not a diagnostic. Stage four is the signal-priority simulation: a SimPy discrete-event simulation runs status-quo versus proposed — meaning the feeder network plus rule-based transit signal priority representing V2X and 802.11p — on real roads with real traffic signal locations.

## 10. Methodology — Layer A

Now Layer A's methodology, and I want to be clear about why this is a graph rather than a simulation. It starts with 18 sourced nodes: five coastal hub cities, three inland hubs and rail junctions, two border crossings, five regional cities, and three ferry destinations — every single one tied to a real historic route, a real signed agreement, or a real dated 2026 launch, not an invented placeholder. Then 18 sourced edges across two states: a status-quo graph with just today's formal buses and informal links, seven edges, versus a proposed graph that adds historic-dormant rail, the Arab Mashreq Railway, the Hejaz Railway revival corridor, and the Jounieh ferry — eighteen edges total. On both graphs, I compute betweenness and closeness centrality using NetworkX, weighted by great-circle distance, which quantifies Beirut's structural role instead of just asserting it in prose. And every number that comes out is a real before-and-after delta from the actual computed graphs — including one negative, counter-intuitive result that I kept and explained rather than dropping, which I'll show you in the results section.

## 11. Technical Building — The Stack

For the technically curious, here's the full stack. GeoPandas and Shapely handle geospatial data wrangling. OSMnx pulls real road and walk networks. H3, Uber's hexagonal spatial index, builds the accessibility grid. PuLP and OR-Tools solve the MCLP optimization. NetworkX runs the graph algorithms and centrality analysis for Layer A. SimPy powers the discrete-event traffic simulation. Folium builds the interactive Leaflet maps. Matplotlib produces the static charts and diagrams you're seeing in this deck. And Nominatim handles real-world geocoding throughout. All of it runs on Python 3.14, backed by a sixteen-out-of-sixteen passing test suite, and the entire pipeline reproduces from a single command.

## 12. Results — GBA-Wide Accessibility

Let's look at real results, starting with Layer B's citywide accessibility. Today, 92.2 percent of the Greater Beirut Area's population is within walking reach of some kind of stop. With the AI-designed feeder network added, that rises to 97.4 percent. Concretely, that's 156,594 additional people reached — and I want to emphasize that number is computed directly from the MCLP optimizer's real output, not an estimate or a round number I picked for the slide.

## 13. Results — Simulated Trip Completion

This next number answers a different, narrower question: does the proposed system actually work when you simulate real riders moving through it? Among people who were previously completely uncovered — literally no route existed for them — simulated trip completion goes from 0 percent to 91.9 percent once they have the feeder-plus-trunk system available. The average simulated door-to-door trip time comes out to about 162 minutes, and that number is pulled upward by one genuinely long intercity corridor — line ML1, at roughly four and a half hours trunk time alone. That's a real long-haul corridor, not a simulation bug, and I want to be upfront about that rather than quietly averaging it away.

## 14. Results — Per-Line Coverage and Speed

Breaking this down per line: across the 7 lines that received a feeder network, 22 feeder stops were placed, covering 68.4 percent — 156,594 out of 228,965 people — of each line's underserved population. On trunk speed, signal priority produces small but real gains specifically where a corridor actually has OpenStreetMap-tagged traffic signals for it to act on — lines B3 and B4 see the clearest gains. Lines B6-ML2 and ML1 show no change at all, because their corridors contain zero real traffic-signal nodes — signal priority literally has nothing to intervene on there. I'm calling that out explicitly as an honest null result, not a bug I'm hiding.

## 15. Results — Layer A Centrality

Now Layer A. This is the headline regional number: if Lebanon's dormant and planned links were restored, Beirut's betweenness centrality in the regional network increases by 400 percent, from about 0.132 to about 0.662. Betweenness centrality measures how often a node sits on the shortest path between two other nodes — it's a structural "bridge" score. So this isn't just saying Beirut gets more connections. It's saying Beirut becomes the node that everything else in the region increasingly has to route through.

## 16. Results — The Network, and an Honest Nuance

And here's a result I want to spend real time on, because I think how you handle a result like this says a lot about the rigor of a project. Beirut's closeness centrality — a different metric, measuring average distance to every reachable node — actually falls, by about 44 percent, from roughly 0.0082 to 0.0046. That is not a bug. Closeness measures average distance to everywhere reachable, and the proposed network makes genuinely distant places reachable for the very first time — Riyadh, Mersin, Larnaca, some of them over 1,500 kilometers away. Adding far-but-newly-reachable places increases the average distance faster than it increases how many places you can reach. The honest takeaway is that reconnecting Lebanon gives it a bridging or gateway role in the region, not a "closer to everything" role — and I chose to report that finding and explain it, rather than quietly drop the metric that didn't confirm my thesis.

## 17. Contributions

So what's actually novel here, pulling it together? First, this is design, not diagnosis — an optimization algorithm that proposes an actual route network, not a model that just flags where problems are. Second, two honestly-different scales: a lightweight graph method for national vision, reconciled with a fully-simulated method for a real pilot, instead of one project overreaching on everything. Third, this is grounded in live regional history — the Hejaz Railway revival and the Jounieh ferry are real 2025-2026 developments, so this project is timed with the region, not speculating about it. Fourth, full reproducibility: one command rebuilds every number and every figure in this entire presentation from source data, backed by a fully passing test suite. Fifth, real bugs, documented rather than erased — five separate real data bugs were found and fixed during actual pipeline runs, and I kept a written record of each one. And sixth, honesty about limits, which gets its own slide next.

## 18. Honesty About Limits

I want to be explicit about what I'm not claiming. On Layer B: the origin-destination demand is representative hub-based flow, not a full four-step travel demand model; feeder route geometry is heuristic stop-chaining, not full vehicle routing; and the congestion multiplier applied to free-flow speeds is a documented, unvalidated placeholder pending a better public source. On Layer A: edge weight is great-circle distance, meaning this is a topological abstraction, not a costed or routing-grade network; the node coordinates were pre-seeded rather than pulled live, because my development sandbox had no route to the geocoding service, and I've flagged that explicitly for live re-verification; and three of Layer B's real trunk lines — ML1, ML4, and B6-ML2 — have unconfirmed regular-operation status per the project's own earlier quality-check work, which I've carried through honestly rather than smoothing over.

## 19. Action Plan

Where does this go next? I've laid out a four-phase roadmap, deliberately tied to real initiatives already underway rather than invented ones. Phase one is this project's Beirut pilot — the feeder network plus signal priority, proven in simulation. Phase two replicates the same AI network-design method around the other coastal hub cities: Tripoli, Saida, Tyre, and Jounieh. Phase three is domestic rail revival — Beirut to the Bekaa to Damascus, and Tripoli to Homs — tied directly to OCFTC, ACTC, and the Ministry of Public Works and Transport. And phase four is regional integration: plugging into the Arab Mashreq Railway and the Hejaz Railway revival that are already being negotiated by real governments right now. The end state across all four phases: public transport, not the private car, becomes the default way to move through Lebanon.

## 20. Reflection

A few honest reflections on the process itself. What was rewarding: watching real numbers come out of real code — 92.2 to 97.4 percent, a 400 percent centrality increase — rather than estimates I made up for a slide. The discipline of descoping three separate times without ever actually losing the original ambition, just relocating it into a vision layer that could stay honest about its own scope. And discovering that the region's own history was already pointing the same direction I wanted to take the project. What was challenging: constantly balancing genuine national-scale ambition against a solo, originally three-week, and later seven-day timeline, without either abandoning the vision or letting it swallow the whole deliverable. Working around a sandboxed development environment with no live route to a geocoding service, which I solved by pre-seeding known-accurate data and documenting precisely why that's low-risk for these particular, well-known places. And reporting a counter-intuitive result — that falling closeness centrality number — honestly, instead of quietly dropping the one metric that didn't confirm the thesis I was hoping for.
